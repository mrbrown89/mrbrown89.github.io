# Cheffin’ IT

Whilst I was at University I started working in a village pub behind the bar to help pay my way. During busy times I helped out washing up in the kitchen. Most people would hate doing pot washing but I loved watching the chefs at work. One summer one of the chefs was on holiday and I was asked to help peel some potatoes and help get ready for lunch service. I loved it! Not long after I started working as a chef in the evenings after university. I learned a lot during this time. I remember one lunch shift peeling potatoes and getting annoyed at the task. One of the chefs was doing the work with me said that even though peeling potatoes isn’t a fun job you should still take pride in your work. That flipped a switch in my head. I become extremely passionate about cooking. I was buying recipe book after recipe book. I was watching as many cooking shows as I could. I can remember watching Gordon Ramsay’s Kitchen Nightmares and the F-word on Channel 4 and All 4. I dreamed of working for the big kids, working at the Fat Duck or one of Ramsay’s restaurants. Each morning before heading off to work or university I used to watch old episodes of Gordon Ramsay’s Boiling Point and, my favourite, old BBC episodes of Marco Pierre White. 

Back in the kitchen, I was always trying to improve. I can remember when I started, roast potatoes on a Sunday were deep fried to get some colour on them and then thrown into the oven. I hated this. So I put the effort in to peel spuds, parboiling them and then roasting them in the oven. I was laughed at by other chefs saying its too much work. Whatever I’m gonna do it! So I did. As a result of that extra effort we got a much better product. I used to put a little bowl of roast potatoes on the bar with a pot of gravy and the regular customers who come in for a Sunday drink loved it! Eventually I did the same thing for Yorkshire puddings. One of our regulars used to bring in store bought frozen Yorkshires as a bit of completion! Being so passionate I wanted to use my own knives so I spent weeks researching the perfect set. I still have them to this day! 

As my degree came to an end I thought I’d carry on cooking so I applied to work in a Michelin starred kitchen.

Now this was proper cooking!

Long hours, small hot kitchens full of people. Lots of passion. Lots of shouting. Lots of ‘YES CHEF!’. It was in this kitchen I learned my craft. How to use a knife properly. How to get my mise en place ready. I was taught that you don’t need loads of tools to accomplish something but to learn the tools you have and master them. Learning from these chefs was incredible. Watching them during service was watching art being made. It was here that what I was seeing in those Marco Pierre White videos started to make sense. Cooking to such a high standard was about craft. You don’t need all of the fancy tools. You don’t need loads of recipes or complicated ways of working. You only need a small amount of tools that you master. You keep things simple. You didn’t need loads of fancy ingredients but what you did make had to be perfect every time. Simplicity is extremely difficult to get right and it meant a lot of hard work to prep but when we hit service, thanks to mastering your tools, learning how to taste as you go and the simplicity of your menu you were able to create true art. 

I left this kitchen to work in one final one before leaving the cooking world behind. This final kitchen that was much closer to home. We didn’t have any stars in this restaurant but we were still cooking to the same standard. I had far more responsibilities in this kitchen but still continued to master my tools. Fond memories of making chicken liver parfait at 7am everyday… 

Fast forward to today and I have now worked in IT for a decade. The lessons I learned in those kitchens never left me. I am still driven by simplicity.

In the same way a good chef does not need a drawer full of gadgets, I prefer a small set of tools that I understand deeply and use well. My workspace is simple. Just a MacBook, my phone and my AirPods. No dual monitors. No mechanical keyboard. No complicated setup.

Most of my work happens in the terminal. Occasionally I will fire up Xcode if vim starts slowing me down, but the goal is always the same. Keep things simple. Know your tools. I try to approach infrastructure in the same way I approached a kitchen. Preparation matters. Discipline matters. Everything should have its place.

As much as possible I manage everything as code. Jamf is managed with Terraform. My NAS is configured with SaltStack. The goal is to reduce complexity and build systems that are predictable and easy to understand. In many ways it reminds me of Marco Pierre White’s philosophy in the kitchen. Strip things back. Focus on the fundamentals. Master the tools you have. For me that craft is infrastructure as code. Building clean automation that does one job properly.

As chefs, we were always taught to taste as we go. How else are you supposed to know if what you are making is any good? It is a constant cycle of refining and improving. I have carried that into IT. Always testing what I build, running scripts in VMs, validating configurations, checking outcomes and refining things until they work exactly as they should.

I once heard Ramsay refer to the stove as being a piano for chefs. I think thats a good way of looking at things. For me now my Mac is my piano. 

Those knives I purchased during my first chef job? I still have them. They followed me through every kitchen I ever worked in.
